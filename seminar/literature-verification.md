## 0. How verification was done — READ FIRST (honest limitations)

The sandbox in which this search was run blocks outbound HTTP to every publisher, repository and Israeli college host (MDPI, Frontiers, SAGE, Springer, Elsevier/ScienceDirect, T&F, PMC, DOAJ, ERIC, OpenAlex, Crossref, openu.ac.il, mofet.macam.ac.il, smkb.ac.il, telhai.ac.il, haifa.ac.il, etc.). Direct `WebFetch`/`curl` of landing pages and PDFs was therefore impossible (every attempt returned `EGRESS_BLOCKED`; only github.com was reachable).

What WAS possible: the `WebSearch` tool returns the search engine's indexed copy of each landing page / PDF (title, authors, venue, abstract text, and on MDPI/Frontiers/SAGE/T&F pages the "Open Access"/"Free Full Text" flags). Every article below was verified by at least one exact-title, domain-restricted search whose result echoed the publisher's own page (or the OA PDF) with matching title/authors/venue. Where the indexed page did not expose a detail (e.g., issue number, page range, or the full author list), that detail is explicitly marked **[VERIFY ON PDF]** rather than guessed.

Open-access status was determined by (a) the journal being fully OA (Frontiers, MDPI Education Sciences/Sustainability, Cogent Education, IJQM, RLT, IRRODL, JMSPH, Discover AI, SSHO, International Journal of Instruction, Chais proceedings, דפים, עיונים בחינוך, גילוי דעת, זמן חינוך, רב-גוונים, חמדעת, חוקרים@הגיל הרך) or (b) an indexed OA copy (PMC mirror, T&F "Full article" page, repository CC-BY copy).

**Before citing, open each URL once yourself** (you are not behind this sandbox) and copy the issue/page details from the PDF where flagged.

Counts of fully verified items: **English: 27** (topics A–J); **Hebrew: 9** in the main list (+ 5 Hebrew candidates awaiting author confirmation in §12).

## 1. TOPIC A — Teachers' perceptions of technology integration (K-12 / elementary)

### A1. Alberola-Mulet, Iglesias-Martínez & Lozano-Cabezas (2021) — qualitative, Spain
1. **APA 7:** Alberola-Mulet, I., Iglesias-Martínez, M. J., & Lozano-Cabezas, I. (2021). Teachers' beliefs about the role of digital educational resources in educational practice: A qualitative study. *Education Sciences, 11*(5), Article 239. https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci11050239
2. **URL/DOI:** https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/11/5/239 ; also OA copy in ERIC: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1297373.pdf
3. **Verification:** domain-restricted search on mdpi.com returned the article page (title, authors, Educ. Sci. 2021, 11(5), 239, published 17 May 2021, open access); ERIC full-text PDF also indexed.
4. **Topics:** A (primary), C.
5. **Summary:** Qualitative study using open-ended, audio-recorded interviews (Jan–Feb 2020) with in-service Early Childhood and Primary Education teachers at a Spanish school (convenience sample; mean age 46, range 28–65; exact n **[VERIFY ON PDF]**). Teachers valued the integration of digital educational resources (DER) into classroom practice, but there was no consensus on the appropriate *level* of integration. Satisfaction with DER was mainly linked to pupils' motivation. Teachers also reported limitations tied to pupils' insufficient digital training and to the need for pedagogical models, experience, ICT competence and professional development.
6. **Quotable (paraphrased):** Teachers see digital resources chiefly as a motivational lever for pupils rather than as a transformer of pedagogy; the disagreement among teachers is not *whether* to integrate but *how much*.

### A2. Akram, Abdelrady, Al-Adwan & Ramzan (2022) — systematic review
1. **APA 7:** Akram, H., Abdelrady, A. H., Al-Adwan, A. S., & Ramzan, M. (2022). Teachers' perceptions of technology integration in teaching-learning practices: A systematic review. *Frontiers in Psychology, 13*, Article 920317. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.920317
2. **URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.920317/full ; PMC mirror: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9207921/
3. **Verification:** frontiersin.org-restricted search returned the article page and the Frontiers PDF; PMC copy indexed (proof of OA).
4. **Topics:** A, G.
5. **Summary:** Systematic review of empirical studies on teachers' perceptions of technology integration in teaching–learning practices (number of included studies **[VERIFY ON PDF]**). Across studies teachers hold positive perceptions: technology-supported teaching is seen as enhancing instruction, making learning "exciting and interactive," and keeping learners motivated. Main obstacles reported: slow internet, power outages, lack of infrastructure, lack of online-teaching experience, and insufficient training. The authors conclude that positive attitudes coexist with structural barriers, so integration depends on institutional support and PD.
6. **Quotable:** Positive teacher attitudes are necessary but not sufficient — infrastructure, experience and training determine whether attitude turns into practice.

### A3. Johler, Krumsvik, Bugge & Helgevold (2022) — case study, Norwegian primary school (also E, H)
1. **APA 7:** Johler, M. M. S., Krumsvik, R. J., Bugge, H. E., & Helgevold, N. (2022). Teachers' perceptions of their role and classroom management practices in a technology rich primary school classroom. *Frontiers in Education, 7*, Article 841385. https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2022.841385
2. **URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2022.841385/full ; PDF: https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2022.841385/pdf ; DOAJ: https://doaj.org/article/b1e9f50b4c3248b8aa8167dc7880d8b2
3. **Verification:** frontiersin.org search returned article page + PDF; DOAJ record indexed; ResearchGate record gives full author list.
4. **Topics:** A, E, H (all three strongly).
5. **Summary:** Case study in a Norwegian primary school where digital technology had been a priority for several years; data from individual and focus-group interviews with teachers, classroom observation and a survey. Teachers' perceived role is described as complex and "contemporary": rapid ICT evolution requires constant updating and re-evaluation of practice to facilitate learning in a physical classroom that has "expanded into the digital space." Classroom management with devices was found to depend on leadership, collegial collaboration, good teacher–pupil relationships, and teachers' willingness to adopt the role of a learner. Highly relevant as a methodological model for a qualitative study of elementary teachers.
6. **Quotable:** In technology-rich primary classrooms the teacher's role becomes that of a learner alongside pupils; classroom management is reframed as relationship- and collaboration-based rather than control-based.

### A4. Masry-Herzallah (2022) — Israel, Jewish/Arab teachers, mixed methods (also F)
1. **APA 7:** Masry-Herzallah, A. (2022). Teachers' perceived effectiveness in online teaching during Covid-19 crisis: Comparing Jewish/Arab teachers in Israel. *International Journal of Instruction, 15*(3), 649–676. https://doi.org/10.29333/iji.2022.15336a
2. **URL:** https://www.e-iji.net/dosyalar/iji_2022_3_36.pdf (journal is free online)
3. **Verification:** search returned the journal PDF ("International Journal of Instruction July 2022, Vol.15, No.3") and citation details (pp. 649–676, DOI); ResearchGate record.
4. **Topics:** F (primary), A, G.
5. **Summary:** Mixed-methods study in Israel: questionnaire to 295 teachers and semi-structured interviews with 22 teachers. Quantitatively, socio-economic status, teacher age, technological competence, frequency of synchronous teaching and professional satisfaction predicted perceived effectiveness of online teaching. Qualitatively, low SES — especially in Arab schools — was described as inhibiting effectiveness because of shortages of computers, internet problems and lack of technological skills/support, particularly in elementary education. The author concludes that the shift online may widen gaps between sectors of Israeli society.
6. **Quotable:** Emergency online teaching did not act as an equaliser; it amplified pre-existing sectoral gaps in Israel, with elementary schools in low-SES Arab communities hit hardest.

## 2. TOPIC B — "Future school", 21st-century skills, digital transformation

### B1. Eradze et al. (2023) — post-pandemic teacher PD for digital innovation (also G)
1. **APA 7:** Eradze, M., De Martino, D., Tinterri, A., Albó, L., Bardone, E., Sunar, A. S., & Dipace, A. (2023). After the pandemic: Teacher professional development for the digital educational innovation. *Education Sciences, 13*(5), Article 432. https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci13050432
2. **URL:** https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/13/5/432 ; DOAJ: https://doaj.org/article/947cf1ab07b246a48b0fb297eea0de92
3. **Verification:** search returned the MDPI page and DOAJ record with full author list and "Vol. 13, no. 5, p. 432 (April 2023)".
4. **Topics:** B, G.
5. **Summary:** Qualitative study with 15 representatives of schools at different levels in Italy, Spain and Turkey, focused on their pandemic experiences and future opportunities for teacher professional development (TPD). The paper re-examines the concepts of teacher agency, networked learning and organisational re-configuration in the light of the emergency. It argues that the pandemic created an opening for digital educational innovation that will close unless TPD is redesigned around agency and networks rather than tool training.
6. **Quotable:** The pandemic was a "window" for innovation; sustaining it requires PD that builds teacher agency and professional networks, not one-off technical courses.

### B2. Amemasor, Oppong, Ghansah, Benuwa & Essel (2025) — systematic review of TPD → digital integration (also G)
1. **APA 7:** Amemasor, S. K., Oppong, S. O., Ghansah, B., Benuwa, B.-B., & Essel, D. D. (2025). A systematic review on the impact of teacher professional development on digital instructional integration and teaching practices. *Frontiers in Education, 10*, Article 1541031. https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2025.1541031
2. **URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1541031/full
3. **Verification:** frontiersin.org search returned the article page and XML; author list shown.
4. **Topics:** B, G.
5. **Summary:** Systematic review of 23 peer-reviewed studies (2020–2024) on components of effective TPD for digital instruction. Effective programmes share collaborative learning environments, hands-on digital training, ongoing mentorship and institutional support. The review documents a persistent gap between the growing demand for digital instructional practice and teachers' preparedness. Effective integration shifts instruction from teacher-centred to interactive, student-driven pedagogies that foster engagement, critical thinking and 21st-century skills.
6. **Quotable:** The bottleneck of the "future school" is not devices but teacher preparedness; TPD works when it is collaborative, hands-on, mentored and institutionally backed.

### B3. Skantz-Åberg, Lantz-Andersson, Lundin & Williams (2022) — conceptualisations of teachers' professional digital competence (also G)
1. **APA 7:** Skantz-Åberg, E., Lantz-Andersson, A., Lundin, M., & Williams, P. (2022). Teachers' professional digital competence: An overview of conceptualisations in the literature. *Cogent Education, 9*(1), Article 2063224. https://doi.org/10.1080/2331186X.2022.2063224
2. **URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2331186X.2022.2063224 ; PDF: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/2331186x.2022.2063224
3. **Verification:** tandfonline.com search returned "Full article" page and PDF (Cogent Education is fully OA).
4. **Topics:** G (primary), B.
5. **Summary:** Literature overview of how teachers' professional digital competence (PDC) was conceptualised in research between 2010 and 2019. Identifies seven aspects of competence, with technological competence the most prominent, alongside pedagogical, content, attitudinal, cultural, critical and professional-engagement dimensions. Argues PDC is broader than tool skills and is context-dependent. Useful for defining "digital competence" in the theoretical framework.
6. **Quotable:** The research literature still foregrounds technological skill, whereas PDC also comprises pedagogical judgement, critical/ethical awareness and professional engagement.

## 3. TOPIC C — Digital tools, motivation, engagement, gamification

### C1. Wang & Tahir (2020) — Kahoot! literature review
1. **APA 7:** Wang, A. I., & Tahir, R. (2020). The effect of using Kahoot! for learning – A literature review. *Computers & Education, 149*, Article 103818. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2020.103818
2. **URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0360131520300208 ; repository copy: https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/39435/
3. **Verification:** search returned the ScienceDirect page, the Goldsmiths repository record, and an NTNU impact document stating the article "is available under a Creative Commons license". (Elsevier page license label **[VERIFY ON PAGE]**.)
4. **Topics:** C.
5. **Summary:** Review of 93 studies on Kahoot! (used by ~50% of US K-12 students). Overall positive effects on learning performance, classroom dynamics, attitudes and anxiety; also reports challenges (technical problems, stress from time pressure, wrong answers, and fatigue when used too often). One of the most cited papers in the journal.
6. **Quotable:** Game-based response systems improve engagement and classroom dynamics, but their effect depends on moderation — overuse produces boredom and competitive stress.

### C2. Li, Ma & Shi (2023) — meta-analysis of gamification
1. **APA 7:** Li, M., Ma, S., & Shi, Y. (2023). Examining the effectiveness of gamification as a tool promoting teaching and learning in educational settings: A meta-analysis. *Frontiers in Psychology, 14*, Article 1253549. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1253549
2. **URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1253549/full ; PMC: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10591086/
3. **Verification:** frontiersin.org search returned article page, PDF and EPUB; PMC mirror indexed.
4. **Topics:** C.
5. **Summary:** Meta-analysis of 41 studies (49 independent samples, >5,071 participants). Overall large effect of gamification on learning outcomes (g = 0.822, 95% CI 0.567–1.078). Significant moderators: learner type, discipline, gamification design principles, duration of the gameful experience, and learning environment. Effects are not uniform — design and duration matter.
6. **Quotable:** Gamification produces a large average effect, but the size depends strongly on who the learners are, how the game elements are designed, and how long the intervention lasts.

### C3. Ramírez Ruiz, Vargas Sanchez & Boude Figueredo (2024) — gamification and school engagement (primary & secondary)
1. **APA 7:** Ramírez Ruiz, J. J., Vargas Sanchez, A. D., & Boude Figueredo, O. R. (2024). Impact of gamification on school engagement: A systematic review. *Frontiers in Education, 9*, Article 1466926. https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2024.1466926
2. **URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1466926/full ; DOAJ: https://doaj.org/article/812a3006c2af437ba7ea7d22d5ac89ae
3. **Verification:** search returned Frontiers page/PDF/EPUB and DOAJ record with authors (Universidad de La Sabana, Colombia), published 6 Dec 2024.
4. **Topics:** C.
5. **Summary:** PRISMA systematic review of 90 gamification interventions in primary and secondary education (2013–2023) aimed at school engagement. Documents growth of the field and the predominance of behavioural-engagement outcomes; reports mostly positive engagement results, with variability by design and age. Specifically school-level (not higher-ed), so directly citable for elementary contexts.
6. **Quotable:** In school settings gamification is most consistently associated with behavioural and emotional engagement; evidence on cognitive engagement and long-term learning remains thinner.

### C4 (optional, proceedings). Wijayanti & Prasadja (2022) — Quizizz and Kahoot! in an elementary school
1. **APA 7:** Wijayanti, S. H., & Prasadja, H. (2022). Quizizz and Kahoot! for online-learning engagement in elementary school. In *Proceedings of the 6th Batusangkar International Conference (BIC 2021)*. EAI. https://doi.org/10.4108/eai.11-10-2021.2319534
2. **URL:** https://eudl.eu/doi/10.4108/eai.11-10-2021.2319534 ; PDF: https://eudl.eu/pdf/10.4108/eai.11-10-2021.2319534
3. **Verification:** search returned the EUDL landing page and PDF. Peer-reviewed conference proceedings, not a journal — use only as a supplementary example.
4. **Topics:** C.
5. **Summary:** Qualitative study of elementary teachers and pupils after a teacher workshop; data from Zoom focus-group interviews and WhatsApp open-ended questions. Teachers and pupils preferred Quizizz to Kahoot! (more question types, question bank, ease of use), though teachers said they would use both for variety. Reported issues: network access, data quota and pupils' dependence on parents' mobile devices.
6. **Quotable:** Even simple quiz games raise participation, but in low-resource homes their use is constrained by connectivity and device ownership.

## 4. TOPIC D — Personalisation, adaptive learning, diversity, assistive technology

### D1. Moltudal, Krumsvik & Høydal (2022) — adaptive learning technology in primary education (mixed methods, Norway)
1. **APA 7:** Moltudal, S. H., Krumsvik, R. J., & Høydal, K. L. (2022). Adaptive learning technology in primary education: Implications for professional teacher knowledge and classroom management. *Frontiers in Education, 7*, Article 830536. https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2022.830536
2. **URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2022.830536/full ; DOAJ: https://doaj.org/article/da60914eeb2541d1ae864fa7c273a155
3. **Verification:** frontiersin.org search returned article page (Feb 2022) with authors and abstract; DOAJ record.
4. **Topics:** D, E, H.
5. **Summary:** Mixed-methods study of upper-primary teachers in a real-life Norwegian context introducing Adaptive Learning Technology (ALT) with built-in learning analytics. Explores through three methodological lenses how teachers reason as they integrate ALT and how it affects professional knowledge and classroom management. ALT is characterised by its capacity to personalise curriculum and experiences for each learner and to support teacher-facilitated learning; the study notes ALT has mostly been researched in higher education and little is known about primary teachers. Reports both advantages (differentiation, diagnostic insight) and disadvantages/dilemmas in primary "learning ecologies".
6. **Quotable:** Adaptive technology does not remove the teacher from differentiation — it moves the teacher's work toward interpreting analytics and orchestrating the classroom.

### D2. Merikko & Kivimäki (2022) — practitioners' views on adaptive technology and the teaching profession (Finland; also H)
1. **APA 7:** Merikko, J., & Kivimäki, V. (2022). "Replacing teachers? Doubt it." Practitioners' views on adaptive learning technologies' impact on the teaching profession. *Frontiers in Education, 7*, Article 1010255. https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2022.1010255
2. **URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2022.1010255/full ; PDF: https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2022.1010255/pdf
3. **Verification:** search returned Frontiers page and PDF ("TYPE Original Research, PUBLISHED 13 October 2022"). First-name initials **[VERIFY ON PDF]**.
4. **Topics:** D, H.
5. **Summary:** Analysed 114 social-media posts by Finnish education practitioners using thematic analysis and epistemic network analysis. Practitioners saw the human teacher as necessary even when adaptive technologies are available; ALT was framed as a support for differentiation and workload, not a replacement. Concerns focused on pedagogy, ethics and the changing nature of the profession.
6. **Quotable:** Practitioners frame adaptive technology as a differentiation assistant; the relational and pedagogical core of teaching is seen as non-automatable.

### D3. Simon & Zeng (2024) — scoping review of teachers' perspectives on adaptive learning technologies
1. **APA 7:** Simon, P. D., & Zeng, L. M. (2024). Behind the scenes of adaptive learning: A scoping review of teachers' perspectives on the use of adaptive learning technologies. *Education Sciences, 14*(12), Article 1413. https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci14121413
2. **URL:** https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/14/12/1413
3. **Verification:** search returned MDPI page and SciProfiles record with authors/affiliations (HKBU, HKU), 14(12), 1413.
4. **Topics:** D.
5. **Summary:** Scoping review of empirical studies on teachers' perspectives regarding adaptive learning technologies (ALT). Teachers report that some ALTs provide diagnostic insights that support targeted differentiation both inside the platform and in whole-class instruction. Identifies teacher training as a key but under-supported condition, along with concerns about data, autonomy and workload. Concludes that teacher perspectives are under-represented relative to student-outcome studies.
6. **Quotable:** Teachers value adaptive systems for the diagnostic information they yield, but report that training and time to act on it are the missing ingredients.

### D4. Navas-Bonilla, Guerra-Arango, Oviedo-Guado & Murillo-Noriega (2025) — inclusive education through technology (systematic review)
1. **APA 7:** Navas-Bonilla, C. del R., Guerra-Arango, J. A., Oviedo-Guado, D. A., & Murillo-Noriega, D. E. (2025). Inclusive education through technology: A systematic review of types, tools and characteristics. *Frontiers in Education, 10*, Article 1527851. https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2025.1527851
2. **URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1527851/full ; PDF: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1527851/pdf
3. **Verification:** frontiersin.org search returned page, PDF and NLM XML with full author list.
4. **Topics:** D.
5. **Summary:** PRISMA systematic review synthesising 159 studies on technology for inclusive education. Maps types of technology (assistive, adaptive, communication, AI-based), the tools used, and their characteristics for learners with diverse needs. Reports that technology supports participation, autonomy and access, while implementation depends on teacher training, accessibility design and infrastructure.
6. **Quotable:** Technology becomes inclusive only when tools are matched to specific learner needs and teachers are trained to use them; the device alone does not create inclusion.

### D5. Mukhtarkyzy et al. (2025) — assistive technologies for SEND students in schools (systematic review)
1. **APA 7:** Mukhtarkyzy, K., Smagulova, L., Tokzhigitova, A., Serikbayeva, N., Sayakov, O., Turkmenbayev, A., & Assilbayeva, R. (2025). A systematic review of the utility of assistive technologies for SEND students in schools. *Frontiers in Education, 10*, Article 1523797. https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2025.1523797
2. **URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1523797/full ; PDF: https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1523797/pdf
3. **Verification:** search returned Frontiers page/PDF/EPUB and ResearchGate record with full author list (published 11 Feb 2025).
4. **Topics:** D.
5. **Summary:** Systematic review of school-based studies on assistive technology (AT) for students with special educational needs and disabilities. AT promotes learning independence and engagement; mobile AT for pupils with ASD improved communication, interpersonal and organisational skills. The authors stress the small number of eligible studies, a lack of evidence on long-term academic outcomes, and the need to study the teaching-technique–technology relationship. Calls for research on physical, hearing and intellectual impairments.
6. **Quotable:** Evidence for AT is promising for participation and independence but thin on long-term achievement; the interaction between the teacher's method and the tool is the under-studied variable.

## 5. TOPIC E — Distraction, screen time, self-regulation, classroom management with devices

(A3 Johler et al. 2022 and D1 Moltudal et al. 2022 are the best *primary-school* sources on classroom management with devices; the items below add the distraction/self-regulation evidence.)

### E1. Pérez-Juárez, González-Ortega & Aguiar-Pérez (2023) — digital distractions (students' view)
1. **APA 7:** Pérez-Juárez, M. Á., González-Ortega, D., & Aguiar-Pérez, J. M. (2023). Digital distractions from the point of view of higher education students. *Sustainability, 15*(7), Article 6044. https://doi.org/10.3390/su15076044
2. **URL:** https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/15/7/6044
3. **Verification:** mdpi.com-restricted search returned the article page with authors and abstract.
4. **Topics:** E.
5. **Summary:** Study during 2021–2022 in an engineering school where technology is fully integrated into classrooms; discussions and surveys with 105 students plus bivariate correlations. Students considered digital distractions to have a significant impact on their performance in lab sessions and self-assessed that performance as "improvable". Most reported using devices for non-academic purposes several times per class. Although higher-education, it is one of the few OA empirical studies isolating classroom digital distraction; cite with the caveat that findings must be transferred cautiously to primary pupils.
6. **Quotable:** When devices are fully integrated into the classroom, learners themselves recognise that non-academic use during lessons degrades their performance.

### E2. Ots, Arro, Aus & Oppi (2026) — school-based interventions for problematic digital technology use (systematic review)
1. **APA 7:** Ots, L., Arro, G., Aus, K., & Oppi, P. (2026). School-based interventions for problematic digital technology use: A systematic review. *Frontiers in Education, 11*, Article 1747573. https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2026.1747573
2. **URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1747573/full
3. **Verification:** search returned Frontiers page and ResearchGate record with author list.
4. **Topics:** E.
5. **Summary:** Systematic review of school-based interventions delivered by school personnel to reduce problematic digital-technology use; five eligible controlled trials (three RCTs, two quasi-experiments; ~10,000 participants). Frames the problem with PISA 2022 data: roughly one in three students reported being distracted in most or all mathematics lessons by their own device use. Evaluates effectiveness and identifies factors underpinning successful outcomes (e.g., teacher delivery, skills-based content, family involvement).
6. **Quotable:** Distraction by pupils' own devices is now a mainstream classroom phenomenon (≈1 in 3 pupils in PISA 2022), yet rigorously evaluated school-delivered interventions remain rare.

### E3. Faza & Lestari (2025) — self-regulated learning in the digital age (systematic review)
1. **APA 7:** Faza, A., & Lestari, I. A. (2025). Self-regulated learning in the digital age: A systematic review of strategies, technologies, benefits, and challenges. *The International Review of Research in Open and Distributed Learning, 26*(2), 23–58. https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v26i2.8119
2. **URL:** https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/8119 ; ERIC: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1477329.pdf
3. **Verification:** search returned the IRRODL page, the journal's APA citation export, and the ERIC full-text PDF.
4. **Topics:** E.
5. **Summary:** Systematic review of SRL strategies and supporting technologies in digital learning environments. Reports that digital devices have become the main source of distraction, which raises the importance of SRL as a countermeasure; maps technologies (dashboards, prompts, planning tools) that scaffold goal-setting, monitoring and reflection, and their benefits and challenges. Useful for the argument that device-rich classrooms require explicit teaching of self-regulation.
6. **Quotable:** Digital environments simultaneously produce the distraction problem and the tools to scaffold self-regulation; the teacher's job is to activate the latter.
